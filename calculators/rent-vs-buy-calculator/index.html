How do you use the rent vs buy calculator?

  1. Enter the price of the home you are looking at and the rent for a similar home nearby.
  2. Set how many years you expect to live there. This matters more than any other input.
  3. Check the down payment and the interest rate (8.5% by default). RBI loan limits are applied for you.
  4. Open “Adjust rates and assumptions” to set the tenure (20 years by default), rent rise, price growth, the return you would earn on savings, yearly owner costs, and the stamp duty, cess, surcharge and registration rates (verified 18 Oct 2026).
  5. Read who ends up ahead, then the table below the calculator to see why.

How does the calculator compare renting and buying?

Both paths start with the same cash: the down payment plus stamp duty and registration. The buyer spends it on the home. The renter invests it. Every month, whoever spends less (EMI plus owner costs, or rent) invests the difference. At the end of your stay the calculator compares net worth.

Worked example: ₹ 1.00 Cr flat or ₹ 30,000 rent, for 10 years

The bank can lend 75% (₹ 75,00,000), so the down payment is ₹ 25,00,000. Stamp duty and registration at 7.6% add ₹ 7,60,000, so the starting cash is ₹ 32,60,000. The EMI at 8.5% for 20 years is ₹ 65,087, about ₹ 35,000 more than the first month’s rent.

With 6% yearly price growth, the flat is worth ₹ 1,79,08,477 after 10 years and ₹ 52,49,537 of the loan is still owed, so the buyer’s net worth is ₹ 1,26,58,940. The renter invests the starting cash and the monthly gap at 10%, with rent rising 5% a year, and ends with ₹ 1,42,04,262. On these assumptions renting is ahead by ₹ 15,45,322.

What does the result mean for your decision?

The calculator answers one question: which path leaves you with more money, if you really invest the difference every month. If you would not invest it, the renter’s figure is too high and buying looks better than shown.

Money is only part of the call. Buying fixes your housing cost once the EMI ends and lets you change the home as you like. Renting keeps you free to move for work or schools. If you lean towards buying, check the total cost of buying a home and your down payment next.

What costs apply when buying in Bengaluru?

On a sale deed in Bengaluru, Karnataka charges 5% stamp duty on the market value (Karnataka Stamp Act, Schedule Art. 20(1)), a 10% cess on the duty (s.3B) and, inside the Greater Bengaluru Area, a 2% surcharge on the duty (Greater Bengaluru Governance Act 2024, s.170). The registration fee is 2% (IGR notification of 29 Aug 2025, in force from 31 Aug 2025). Together that is 7.6% of the value. The first sale of a flat pays less duty: 2% up to ₹ 20 L and 3% above ₹ 20 L up to ₹ 45 L, so the total is 4.24% or 5.36%. Resale flats, plots and houses pay 5% at any price. Rates verified 18 Oct 2026 · confirm on Kaveri Online before registering. The calculator uses 7.6% by default; change it if your Sub-Registrar quotes a different amount.

RBI rules cap the home loan at 90% of the value for loans up to ₹ 30 L, 80% for loans above ₹ 30 L and up to ₹ 75 L, and 75% above ₹ 75 L. The calculator applies these caps.

What does this calculator leave out?

  • Income-tax benefits on home loans and tax on investment returns. These depend on your tax position; ask your CA.
  • Costs of selling the home at the end, and brokerage on either side.
  • GST on an under-construction home. Add it to the stamp duty figure if it applies to you.
  • Maintenance, property tax and repairs, unless you enter them as owner’s yearly costs.
  • Changes in interest rates during the loan.

Frequently asked questions

Whether renting or buying is better in Bengaluru depends on how long you stay, how fast prices grow and what return you earn on savings. For a ₹ 1 crore flat against ₹ 30,000 rent over 10 years, with 6% growth and a 10% return, renting comes out ahead by about ₹ 15.45 L. With 8% growth and an 8% return, buying wins.

Buying usually needs a long stay to beat renting, because stamp duty and registration (about 7.6% in Bengaluru) and early loan interest have to be earned back through price growth. The calculator shows the year buying catches up for your inputs. If it says “Not in 30 years”, the assumptions favour renting throughout.

The rent vs buy comparison is only fair if both households start with the same money. The buyer puts the down payment and stamp duty into the home, so the renter is assumed to invest that same amount, plus the monthly gap between EMI and rent. If you would spend that money instead, buying looks better than the result shows.

No one can promise a price growth rate for a Bengaluru home, so the calculator treats it as your assumption, set at 6% a year by default. Try a lower and a higher rate and look at the table of results. If buying only wins at a high growth rate, the purchase depends on prices rising quickly.
